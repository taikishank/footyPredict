# Market-Derived Features, Calibration, and Market-Blending for v4

## What has this project already tried regarding betting-market features, and why did it fail/succeed? Was the market ever blended in, or only benchmarked?

### Takeaway
The market has been used **exclusively as a benchmark/target**, never as a model input or blended-in probability. The project measured the gap to Pinnacle's de-vigged closing line (0.0211 log loss, ~2.8x the entire ratings phase's gain) and diagnosed it as **missing signal, not a calibration defect** — temperature scaling recovered nothing (in fact cost 0.0004), which rules out the "just recalibrate the model's output" shortcut. No commit or finding shows market probabilities ever entering the feature matrix or being logistically blended with model output.

### Cited Findings
- `ml/FINDINGS_MARKET.md`: "The gap to the closing line is 0.0211 — about 2.8× everything the ratings phase bought." Table: class priors 1.07067, v2 (served) 0.99178, +attack/defence (best arm) 0.98402, Pinnacle closing de-vigged 0.96293 — model closes 80.4% of the gap from no-information to the market. — `/home/taiki/Desktop/projects/footyPredict/ml/FINDINGS_MARKET.md` (lines 26-64)
- Paired bootstrap: model (atkdef) trails market by −0.02109 log loss, 95% CI [−0.02387, −0.01828], P(model better) = 0.000 — i.e., statistically certain the market still beats the model on identical fixtures. — same file, lines 44-47
- "The gap is information, not calibration": marginal outcome rates are nearly identical between model and market (home 0.4522 vs 0.4440 vs actual 0.4412), but the model's probabilities are *compressed toward the base rate* — 3.7pts too high at the bottom decile, 10.1pts too low at the top decile of market-ranked P(away); the market's separation (mean P(away) difference between away-win and non-away-win fixtures) is +0.1501 vs the model's +0.1204, i.e. the market discriminates ~25% better. — same file, lines 68-100
- Temperature scaling test: fit on first half of validation rows, applied to second half. T=0.945 (mild sharpen); held-out raw loss 0.98674 → tempered 0.98713, i.e. **−0.00039, worse**. Conclusion: "A monotone transform of the model's own output recovers *nothing*... The missing thing is signal, not a scaling constant." This explicitly "kills two shortcuts... a calibration layer bolted onto v2, and the assumption... that the v2 hyperparameters are simply over-regularised." — same file, lines 102-120
- The gap by outcome: home_win gap +0.00130 (2.7% of total gap — model essentially level with market here), draw +0.02981 (35.5%), away_win +0.04236 (**61.8%**, the largest single component). By market one-sidedness, the gap is roughly flat (16.6%/23.8%/24.8%/20.9%/13.9% across five buckets), meaning it's a general deficiency, not confined to lopsided or close fixtures. — same file, lines 124-156
- Open item 1 in the findings names "away-specific team strength" as "now the best-evidenced hypothesis," since nothing in the feature set distinguishes a club's home form from away form (the venue-strength "atkdef" carries one home-advantage parameter per league, not per club). — same file, lines 254-260
- Cost investigation: The Odds API (already used live, `ingestor-go/internal/odds`) was priced and rejected for historical backfill — its archive starts 2020-06-06 (56.6% coverage), historical snapshots cost 10 quota credits vs 1 for live, ~36,900 credits needed (the $59/month tier), and the free tier returns `HTTP 401 HISTORICAL_UNAVAILABLE_ON_FREE_USAGE_PLAN`. football-data.co.uk was adopted instead: free, no key, 96% coverage of the validation span with Pinnacle closing prices vs 57% at $59/month. — same file, lines 160-188; `ml/build_market_index.py` lines 1-18
- Two de-vig methods (multiplicative and Shin's) are both implemented and compared; they differ by only 0.00016 on Pinnacle's ~2.5% margin, so "the de-vig choice is not load-bearing here," though it's noted this would diverge more at Bet365's higher 5.19% margin. — `ml/FINDINGS_MARKET.md` lines 189-196; confirmed in code at `ml/market_lib.py` (Shin's method implemented via bisection, lines 56-117)
- Overround (bookmaker margin) columns correlate with fixture date (Pinnacle closing +0.542, Bet365 open +0.356 — margins have widened over time in this archive) but this is recorded only as a diagnostic; margin is "never scored and never fed to a model." No margin/overround feature has been tried as a predictive signal. — same file, lines 236-251
- Grading path (`backtest/grade_calls.py`, `backtest/report.py`) computes a live model-vs-market Brier score on graded fixtures over "a few weeks of rows," used only for scorecarding, not fitting. Findings note nobody has yet checked whether this live comparison agrees in direction with the offline archive benchmark (open item 6). — same file, lines 285-289
- In serving code, market/odds probabilities are used only to compute a display "edge" (`model_probs[outcome] - market_probs[outcome]`) for flagging picks, and to persist a snapshot for later grading — not as a model input feature. — `/home/taiki/Desktop/projects/footyPredict/inference-py/app/main.py` lines 209-263

### Inferences
- Because the compression/under-confidence problem survived a monotone post-hoc transform (temperature scaling), any future calibration attempt using only the model's own output (Platt scaling, isotonic regression on the model's own probabilities) is very likely to fail for the same reason — these are also monotone transforms of a single input. The finding effectively pre-empts trying Platt/isotonic on model-only output as a v4 lever.
- The market was never given a chance to add value as a *feature/blend* (as opposed to purely a benchmark) — this is a live, untried avenue given the explicit finding that the gap is "information the model doesn't have," some of which the market's price *does* encode (e.g., lineups/team news it never sees per open item 4).
- Since the deficiency concentrates on away wins and the draw and is roughly flat by market one-sidedness, a market-blend feature is more likely to help by injecting the market's superior *discrimination* into rows with real away-strength uncertainty, rather than uniformly recalibrating everything.

### Gaps
- No internal test of blending market probability with model probability (e.g., log-odds averaging or a stacked logistic regression) exists yet in this codebase — cannot report an internal result for this, only that it hasn't been tried.
- Open item 6 (does the live model-vs-market Brier from `backtest/report.py` agree with the archive benchmark direction) is unresolved as of the last findings update.

## Is there evidence of closing-line value (CLV), market movement/steam, or multi-bookmaker consensus/de-vigging having been tried?

### Takeaway
De-vigging has been tried and validated (two methods, negligible difference at Pinnacle's margin). **Multi-bookmaker consensus, market movement/steam (open-vs-close delta as a feature), and closing-line value as a *bettor-skill* metric have not been tried** in this codebase — the project only uses a single point-in-time snapshot (closing price for the benchmark, and whatever price the odds poller captures live) rather than any temporal or cross-bookmaker signal.

### Cited Findings
- De-vigging: both `devig_multiplicative` and `devig_shin` are implemented in `ml/market_lib.py`, motivated by the fact that "bookmakers load proportionally more margin onto longshots... which would understate favourites and land exactly on the draw" (favorite-longshot bias in margin allocation) — and Shin's method explicitly corrects for this by modeling an "insider betting" fraction `z`. — `/home/taiki/Desktop/projects/footyPredict/ml/market_lib.py` lines 12-21, 56-80
- `build_market_index.py`'s `PRICE_SETS` pulls four columns: Pinnacle closing, Pinnacle opening, market-average closing, and Bet365 opening — so opening vs. closing price *data* already exists in the pipeline (`("pinnacle_closing", ...)`, `("pinnacle_open", ...)`) but findings show no feature computed from the *difference* between them (no steam/movement feature). — `/home/taiki/Desktop/projects/footyPredict/ml/build_market_index.py` lines 74-82
- `market_benchmark.py` supports `--source pinnacle_open` as an alternative benchmark run, confirming opening-line comparison exists as a benchmark option, but there is no finding comparing opening-vs-closing accuracy gap (which would itself be a measure of how much the market moves on information between open and close) — this analysis is not present in `FINDINGS_MARKET.md`.
- Market-average closing coverage is only 63.0% in the archive vs 95.7% for Pinnacle closing (`ml/FINDINGS_MARKET.md` table, line 180-186) — so a true multi-bookmaker consensus feature would have material missing-data cost with the current free data source.
- Grep of `inference-py/app` and `ml/*.py` and `backtest/*.py` for "closing line value," "steam," "consensus" found no matches; the only calibration-adjacent uses of "market" are the benchmark (`ml/market_benchmark.py`), the archive builder (`ml/build_market_index.py`), and the live edge display (`inference-py/app/main.py`).

### Inferences
- Because opening and closing Pinnacle prices are already downloaded and joined (just not differenced), an open-to-close movement feature (steam) is cheap to prototype from existing data — no new ingestion needed, unlike a true multi-bookmaker consensus feature which is currently gated by the free data source's 63% coverage for market-average odds.
- The ingestor's live odds poller (`ingestor-go/internal/odds`) already polls repeatedly pre-kickoff (implied by "edges is an append-only poll-by-poll log" in commit `27c30e8`), meaning the *live* system already has the raw material for a movement/steam signal (successive snapshots) even though the offline archive benchmark only has two static points (open/close) per fixture. These are two different feature-engineering opportunities with different data footprints.

### Gaps
- No internal evidence either way on whether steam/movement would help this specific project's log loss — this is an untried external technique (see next question's findings for the literature).
- Whether the ingestor's poll-by-poll live log is dense enough (polling frequency) to detect genuine "steam" (rapid synchronized moves) as opposed to just gradual drift is not documented in the files read.

## What calibration techniques (Platt, isotonic, temperature scaling, beta calibration) has this project applied, and what remains untried?

### Takeaway
Only **temperature scaling** has been formally tried (and rejected, net negative) and a **reliability/calibration curve plot** (via `sklearn.calibration.calibration_curve`) exists as a diagnostic in `ml/train.py`, logged to MLflow — it is a visualization, not a correction applied to served probabilities. **Platt scaling, isotonic regression, and beta calibration have not been applied anywhere in this codebase.**

### Cited Findings
- `ml/train.py` imports `sklearn.calibration.calibration_curve`, defines `plot_calibration()`, and logs the resulting PNG (`calibration_curve.png`) as an MLflow artifact after validation (lines 12, 17, 41-55, 101-102) — diagnostic only, output is not fed back into `predict_proba`.
- Temperature scaling was tried exactly once, in the market-benchmark phase, fit on the first half of validation rows and applied to the second: T=0.945, raw held-out loss 0.98674 → tempered 0.98713 (worse by 0.00039). This is the project's only attempt at a post-hoc probability recalibration method, and it failed. — `ml/FINDINGS_MARKET.md` lines 102-114
- No occurrences of "platt," "isotonic," or "beta calibration" appear anywhere in `ml/`, `inference-py/app/`, or `backtest/` (confirmed by grep across those directories).
- The finding explicitly frames why a broader recalibration approach is a dead end for the *current* compression problem: "the compression is not a global over-confidence setting that a recalibration layer could undo... it does not identify those fixtures as extreme in the first place. The missing thing is signal, not a scaling constant." — same file, lines 110-113

### Inferences
- Because the diagnosed problem is under-*discrimination* (not miscalibration in the strict statistical sense — marginal rates match), classic univariate recalibration methods (Platt/isotonic/temperature/beta) that only reshape an existing scalar score are all pre-tested-by-implication to fail here, since temperature scaling is representative of that whole family for a well-separated (already near-linear) 3-class softmax output. Isotonic or beta calibration might behave slightly differently since they're non-parametric/asymmetric, but the finding's own logic (a monotone transform of one already-computed probability cannot manufacture new discrimination) applies to all of them equally.
- The genuinely untried class of calibration technique — using the **market's probability as an external calibration target or blending input** (rather than recalibrating the model's own scalar) — is fundamentally different in kind from what's been tried, because it introduces *new information* rather than reshaping existing information. This is the one calibration-adjacent idea not ruled out by the temperature-scaling finding.

### Gaps
- No test of isotonic regression or beta calibration specifically was run internally; the temperature-scaling finding is the only direct evidence, and while its logic likely generalizes, this project has not empirically confirmed isotonic/beta calibration also fail.
- No multiclass-specific calibration (e.g., Dirichlet calibration, which generalizes beta calibration to K>2 classes) has been tried or discussed.

## What external, well-evidenced techniques has this project NOT tried (market as prior/shrinkage target, logistic blending, market-movement signal, multi-bookmaker consensus, market-implied targets), and what's the evidence each improves log loss?

### Takeaway
The strongest, most directly relevant external technique is **logistic/log-odds blending of model output with de-vigged market probability** (stacking), which shows up in the literature as producing calibrated, market-anchored ensembles; **multi-bookmaker consensus averaging** has documented accuracy gains over single-bookmaker or model-only approaches; **favorite-longshot bias correction via Shin's de-vig** is already adopted here and is standard practice. Market-movement/steam and CLV-as-a-feature (as opposed to CLV-as-a-bettor-skill-metric) have very little direct academic literature specific to improving a *statistical model's* log loss — most CLV literature addresses evaluating *bettor* skill, not model features, so this is a genuine evidence gap, not a validated technique.

### Cited Findings
- An isotonic-logistic-regression meta-learner that includes bookmaker closing odds as an input, blending outputs into final probabilities anchored to market consensus, is described as representing "the current state of the art in match outcome forecasting" among practitioner ensemble approaches. — [Football Match Prediction Algorithm: A Practitioner's Guide](https://www.betsyscore.com/blog/football-match-prediction-algorithm) (practitioner source, not peer-reviewed — treat as industry practice, not proven research)
- "The Betting Odds Rating System" (ELO-Odds) paper: replacing the *actual match result* with the betting-odds-implied probability inside an Elo-style rating update produces a rating system that significantly outperforms a goals-based or results-based Elo (p<0.01 vs. ELO-Goals), because "betting odds known prior to a match... possess more information than the result known after the match." This is a different mechanism from blending final probabilities — it uses the market as a *training target/pseudo-outcome* for a rating system, not as a feature at inference time. Note: the paper does not claim to beat raw betting odds themselves, and does not test hybrid probability blending. — [The Betting Odds Rating System (PLOS ONE)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0198668)
- Multi-bookmaker consensus: averaging odds across many bookmakers "cancel[s] out strategic and unintentional inefficiencies of individual bookmakers"; a rule based on five online bookmakers' consensus predicted 77–79% of tennis matches correctly vs. 72% for ATP rankings alone (tennis, not football, but same consensus-averaging mechanism). — [Wisdom-of-crowds sports forecasting research summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/wisdom-of-ignorant-crowds-predicting-sport-outcomes-by-mere-recognition/7C2AC74AD203E72246B69F340E274599)
- Aggregated sports-tipster crowd predictions "contain information not in betting prices," with a strategy of betting when a majority of tipsters agree producing average returns of 1.317% across 68,339 events — but the same source notes crowd wisdom shows "limited profitability" when tested head-to-head against betting odds. — [The wisdom of amateur crowds (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0377221718306209)
- Pinnacle's closing line has historically been extremely well-calibrated: one large-sample study of 397,935 Pinnacle-priced games found r²=0.997 correlation between closing-line implied probability and observed outcome frequency. — [The Efficiency of the Pinnacle.com Closing Line](https://www.football-data.co.uk/blog/pinnacle_efficiency.php) (this project's own data-source vendor's blog; directionally consistent with, but a different measurement from, the project's internal 0.021 log-loss gap finding)
- However, a pre-registered, non-peer-reviewed independent study (23,500 matches, six European leagues, 2016/17–2025/26, holdout methodology) found Pinnacle's closing-line calibration **deteriorated in the most recent holdout period (2023/24–2025/26)**: calibration slope moved from 1.044/intercept +0.028 (2016/17–2022/23) to slope 1.096/intercept +0.062, with longshots winning "markedly less often than priced" (372 actual wins vs. 422.5 expected, ~2.6 standard deviations below projection) in every season and league of the holdout. The authors could not rule out that this is simply an unlucky draw rather than a persistent shift, and call for prospective validation. — [Pre-registered test of bookmaker pricing efficiency, GitHub](https://github.com/urajimiruliss-arch/football-market-efficiency) (caveat: independent/unaffiliated GitHub repo, not a peer-reviewed publication; treat as a single unverified study, not established fact)
- CLV literature is almost entirely framed around evaluating *bettor* decision quality ("positive CLV is one of the strongest signals that a bettor is finding real value... separates skill from luck"), not as a feature engineering technique for a statistical forecasting model. — [What Is Closing Line Value? (Pikkit)](https://pikkit.com/blog/what-is-closing-line-value); [Closing line: the most important metric in sports trading (Trademate Sports)](https://tradematesports.medium.com/closing-line-the-most-important-metric-in-sports-trading-58e56cdb4458)
- Line-movement/"steam" content found was exclusively bettor-facing guidance (how to detect sharp money, how much a "true steam move" typically shifts a spread) rather than any academic study quantifying whether open-to-close price movement, used as a *predictive feature* in a statistical model, reduces log loss. — [Steam Moves Betting Explained (SportBot AI)](https://www.sportbotai.com/blog/steam-moves-betting-explained-ai-data-insights); [Line Movement in Betting (OddsIndex)](https://oddsindex.com/guides/line-movement-betting) (both marketing/blog content, low evidentiary weight)
- On prediction markets generally (Kalshi, not football, so directly analogous mechanism but a different market/vertical): calibration is time-varying — "calibration parameters sit at their perfect-calibration reference values in the middle of a contract's life but depart sharply as expiry approaches" — and both this time-varying miscalibration and parlay overpricing were found to be systematic and "admit computational correction." This supports the general principle that market prices are not a fixed, uniformly-reliable target and that *when* in the market's lifecycle a price is sampled matters. — [Prices, Probabilities, and Parlays (arXiv 2607.14430)](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14430)

### Inferences
- **Logistic/log-odds blending (stacking model output with de-vigged market probability)** is the technique with the clearest mechanism and the most direct fit to this project's own diagnosis: the project's own finding is that the model under-discriminates while the market over-discriminates by ~25% on the same fixtures, which is exactly the situation a weighted blend (e.g., `logit(p_final) = a + b1*logit(p_model) + b2*logit(p_market)`, fit by logistic regression on held-out folds) is designed to fix — it lets the market's superior discrimination pull the model's compressed extremes outward, and the two-coefficient fit is a strict superset of "ignore the model" (b1=0) and "ignore the market" (b2=0), so it cannot make things worse on the fitting set and should be validated on a genuinely held-out period. This is a **feature engineering / ensembling idea, not literature-proven for this exact project**, but it directly targets the diagnosed mechanism (compression) in a way plain recalibration (proven not to work) cannot.
- **Market-implied probability as an input feature at inference time carries real leakage risk** that must be handled carefully: the closing line is only available *after* the market has absorbed all information up to kickoff, so a feature at that vintage is fine for kickoff-time inference but leaks information relative to any pipeline that wants to predict earlier (e.g., for the "Upcoming Fixtures" UI shown before kickoff, where the correct market price to use is whatever is available *at prediction time*, not the closing line). The project's own opening-vs-closing distinction (already downloaded) is the natural guardrail: train/validate the blend using **opening** or an early-snapshot price as the "feature" version of market information, and reserve **closing** price strictly for the benchmark/target role it already has, to avoid quietly training on look-ahead information that a live system won't have at serve time. This mirrors the exact leak-check discipline the project already applies elsewhere (commit `1f516d3`, "Reconstruct v2 edges for kicked-off fixtures, with the leak check enforced").
- **Favorite-longshot bias correction (Shin's de-vig)** is validated technique already adopted; the independent efficiency study found the *direction* of favorite-longshot mispricing (longshots overpriced relative to true probability) may have gotten *stronger* in 2023-2026 — if true, this slightly increases the value of Shin-style correction over multiplicative de-vig in more recent seasons, though the project's own measurement (0.00016 difference, "not load-bearing") is specific to Pinnacle's low ~2.5% margin and might not generalize to the higher-margin sources (Bet365 5.19%) if those were ever used as a live-time feature source instead of Pinnacle closing.
- **Multi-bookmaker consensus** has real supporting evidence in adjacent sports (tennis) but the project's own coverage numbers make it a second-order improvement at best here: market-average-closing coverage is only 63% vs. 95.7% for Pinnacle alone in the free archive, so pursuing this would either sacrifice coverage or require paying for a broader data source — likely not worth it before the higher-leverage blend/away-strength work.
- **Steam/movement-as-a-feature** has no strong external evidence of directly improving a statistical model's log loss (all sourced material addresses bettor timing, not model features) — this should be flagged as a plausible-but-unvalidated idea, worth a cheap internal ablation (open-to-close delta, already available in the archived data) rather than something to invest heavily in based on outside evidence.
- **Market-implied probability as a shrinkage/regularizing prior** (e.g., a Bayesian approach where team-strength parameters are pulled toward what the market implies, rather than the market entering as a raw feature) has plausible mechanism (Empirical-Bayes-style shrinkage reduces overreaction to small samples, a documented failure mode in sports betting model backtests) but no football-specific study was found quantifying its log-loss benefit; the clearest documented instance found (ELO-Odds) uses the market as a *training pseudo-target* for an Elo-style updater, which is a related-but-distinct idea worth distinguishing from "shrink team ratings toward market-implied strength."

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed, football-specific study was found quantifying the log-loss (or Brier) improvement specifically from logistic blending of a standalone statistical model's output with de-vigged market probability. The closest analog found (ELO-Odds) blends the market into *rating updates*, not into final probabilities via stacking — different mechanism.
- No football-specific study was found on open-to-close market movement as a standalone predictive *feature* for a match-outcome model (as distinct from CLV-as-bettor-skill-metric). This is an evidence gap, not a negative result — the idea may still be worth a cheap internal test given the data is already downloaded, but it should not be oversold as "literature-proven."
- The independent pre-registered market-efficiency study (GitHub, unaffiliated author) is the only source found addressing whether Pinnacle's closing-line efficiency is stable over time; it is not peer-reviewed and its authors themselves flag inconclusiveness (could be an unlucky draw). Treat its "efficiency may be degrading" claim as a lead worth investigating on the project's own more recent seasons (it already has 2024-26 data in `ml/data/raw/market`), not as settled fact.
- The `arxiv.org/pdf/1802.08848` paper ("Combining historical data and bookmakers' odds in modelling football scores") could not be read — WebFetch returned raw undecoded PDF binary content rather than text — so its specific method and quantitative result are not verified here. This is a promising-titled source that should be retried with a proper PDF text extraction if the report writer wants stronger academic backing for the blending idea; it is not cited above because it could not actually be read.

## Practically: what's the honest, realistic ceiling on improvement from market-blending vs. improving the underlying model?

### Takeaway
The project's own numbers set a hard, already-measured ceiling: **the market beats the current best model arm by 0.02109 log loss (95% CI [−0.02387, −0.01828]), with P(model better) = 0.000** — that is the entire pool of achievable gain from "catching up to the market" by any means, blending included, and even that ceiling is explicitly a *lower bound* on the true, information-complete ceiling, since the closing line prices in lineups/team news the model doesn't see (Phase 9's lineup backfill is the project's only current attempt at that). Blending cannot exceed this pool; realistically it captures some fraction of it (likely a meaningful fraction, since it directly targets the diagnosed discrimination gap, but not all of it, since some of the market's edge is information the model structurally lacks).

### Cited Findings
- Ceiling quantified: "the entire ratings phase moved the model from 73.2% to 80.4% of the way to the market — 7.2 points of the 26.8 that were open. 19.6 points remain," and "three or four more findings the size of the ones already banked would reach the closing line" — i.e., the project's own estimate is that reaching full market parity is roughly 3-4 comparable feature wins away, not one blend away. — `ml/FINDINGS_MARKET.md` lines 49-64
- Explicit statement that the benchmark is a floor, not a ceiling: "The benchmark is a lower bound on what is achievable, not an upper one. Pinnacle's closing line incorporates team news, lineups and money the model never sees. Matching it is not the ceiling of what is possible; it is the ceiling of what is possible without that information." — same file, lines 273-277
- Large-sample historical evidence that Pinnacle specifically is very hard to beat as a standalone forecast (r²=0.997 correlation between closing implied probability and outcome frequency across 397,935 games) reinforces that "beat the market outright" is not a realistic target; "close most of the gap to it" is the more calibrated goal, consistent with the project's own framing. — [The Efficiency of the Pinnacle.com Closing Line](https://www.football-data.co.uk/blog/pinnacle_efficiency.php)

### Inferences
- Market-blending's realistic ceiling should be read as: it can plausibly recover a meaningful share of the 0.021 gap (since it directly attacks the diagnosed compression/discrimination problem in a way pure model feature work has been trying to attack indirectly via away-strength ratings), but it should not be expected, on its own, to *close* the whole gap — some of that 0.021 reflects information (team news, lineups, in-play money) genuinely unavailable to the standalone statistical model, which no amount of clever blending of the model's *own* output with the market recovers beyond what the market's price itself already contains. In other words: blending changes the model's own accuracy toward the market's, but a blended forecast that is 100% market-weighted is definitionally capped at the market's own log loss (0.96293 in this benchmark) — it cannot beat the market using only the market's own information.
- The most honest framing for a report to leadership/stakeholders: model-side feature work (e.g., away-specific ratings, the project's own top open item) and market-blending are **not competing uses of effort** — they attack different parts of the same 0.021 budget. Blending is comparatively cheap to prototype (the market data is already ingested and de-vigged) and should be tried in parallel with, not instead of, continued feature work, precisely because the project's own evidence shows recalibration-only approaches (temperature scaling) fail — meaning the "cheap win" is not recalibration but *combination with new information* (the market's own price), and that's a fundamentally different, still-open door.

### Gaps
- No internal experiment yet quantifies what fraction of the 0.021 gap a logistic blend with the market would actually close for this dataset — this is the single most valuable and cheapest next experiment implied by everything above, and it remains untested.
- No external literature was found giving a generalizable expected-log-loss-reduction number for "blend of standalone model + market" specifically in football 1X2 markets (as opposed to point-spread/totals markets in US sports, which have a larger literature) — this quantitative ceiling has to be measured internally rather than assumed from outside sources.
